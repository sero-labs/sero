Sprite Studio — prototype

A page in the Design Library that turns a character, or a picture of one, into finished sprite sheets. You describe the animations you want in plain words. The AI plans them, a video model draws the movement, and deterministic code makes it real pixel art.

Every sprite on this page is real output from the spike, not a mock-up. The explorer was recovered from the reference image at his true size of 62 × 136 art pixels, and the whip attack came back from Seedance 2.0 through the cleanup pipeline.

The two checkpoints — state 2 and state 5 — are the only places the work stops for you.

1 · Characters — the shelf

A character owns its palette, its size and its anchor, and every animation belongs to it. That ownership is what lets you come back next week, ask for a jump, and get a sprite that matches. Cards lead with the sprite; counts and palettes stay quiet underneath.

2 · Character sheet — the first checkpoint

Ingestion measures the reference rather than guessing at it. The grid, the true size, the palette and the foot line are all found by code, and shown side by side with the original so you can see nothing was lost. Nothing is generated until you approve this. The palette can be capped here — to 32, to 16, or to a fixed set you supply — and the character is re-quantised so you see the result before approving. Height and palette are inherited by every animation.

3 · Ask for animations — plain words in, a plan back

You write what you want. The AI turns it into a plan with a frame count, a play rate and a canvas for each animation, and you can change any of it before a penny is spent. Frame count and play rate are separate: 30 fps is how fast it plays, not how many drawings exist. The plan also states where the feet leave the ground, which is what stops a jump being pinned to the floor — and the runtime checks that claim against the pixels rather than believing it.

The video model is chosen here, in the open, not buried in settings — it changes the result more than any other control, and the two we measured fail in opposite directions. The choice is remembered for next time.

4 · Animation workbench — playback owns the canvas

The sprite is the interface. Playback runs at the animation's real rate, the frame strip sits underneath at working size, and the numbers that matter stay as small chips rather than a panel of dials. Selecting a frame opens its panel on the right, and that panel is the way in to both kinds of fixing — paint it yourself, or tell the AI what is wrong. Both are offered on every frame, whether or not the checks passed.

5 · Animation checkpoint — what was made, and what was fixed

The second and last gate. The sequence is presented finished, with the repairs already done and declared — the run does not stop to ask permission for each one. The report is written in measurements, so approving is a judgement about the art, not about the machinery.

6 · Frame editor — small fixes, nothing more

Reached from ✏ edit on any frame, or by double-clicking it. Deliberately minimal: pencil, eraser, eyedropper, fill, undo, onion skin. Colours come from the character's palette only, so a hand edit cannot break the thing the whole pipeline works to guarantee. It appears in place of the playback stage, so no new window and no new chrome.

7 · Export — a sheet and an atlas any engine can read

One PNG and one Aseprite JSON file. The scale must be a whole number or the pixels blur, so a request that does not divide cleanly is resolved to the nearest one and the real size is stated. The anchor and the palette travel inside the atlas, so a game does not have to be told where his feet are.
